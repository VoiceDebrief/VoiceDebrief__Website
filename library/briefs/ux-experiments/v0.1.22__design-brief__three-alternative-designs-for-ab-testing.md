# Design brief — three alternative designs, shipped alongside the current one, for A/B testing

**To:** Claude Design (or any designer picking this up)
**From:** Dinis Cruz, via the Conductor
**Date:** 8 August 2026 · repo version `v0.1.22`
**Status:** brief — nothing here exists yet

---

## The ask, in one paragraph

Design **three alternative experiences** for the WhatsApp voice note transcription
tool — not three skins of what exists, but three genuinely different bets about how
this product should work. All three ship to the site *in addition to* the current
design, so that four variants run side by side and we can find out which one people
actually get value from. **You may change everything**: layout, information
architecture, the order things happen in, what appears on screen at all, the words,
the colour, the motion, whether there is a landing page, whether the app is one
screen or five. What matters is finding the best experience for real users. Nothing
in the current design is precious.

---

## What the product is

Drop in a WhatsApp voice note (`.opus` / `.ogg` / `.m4a`) → get a transcript, a
summary, and optionally an infographic, in a single pass. Everything runs in the
browser. There is no backend, no account, and nothing stored server-side. The user
brings their own [OpenRouter](https://openrouter.ai/keys) key, which never leaves
their browser.

There is also a **chat pane** where the user can talk to the materials that were
produced — and, more importantly, *drive the tool from the conversation*: ask it to
redraw the infographic with a different model, rewrite the summary in Spanish, and
so on. Commercially this is the most interesting thing in the product, and in the
current design it is hidden behind a tab on the right edge.

## What is genuinely fixed

These are architecture, not taste. Everything else is yours.

1. **No backend.** Static hosting. No server-side rendering, no API of our own, no
   session storage on a server. If a design needs a server, it is out of scope —
   though say so if you think that is the wrong constraint.
2. **Bring your own key.** The OpenRouter key lives in `localStorage` and is used
   directly from the browser. A design may change *when* and *how* we ask for it —
   that is one of the most interesting things to redesign — but not that we need it.
3. **Privacy honesty.** Routed mode means a third-party provider processes the
   recording. That must be visible where the user makes the choice, never buried in
   a footer. You may redesign how it is communicated; you may not make it quieter.
4. **The engine API is stable.** All four designs consume the same `window.__tool`
   actions (see `SKILL__api.md`). You are redesigning the experience, not the
   pipeline. If a design needs a new action, that is a legitimate finding — write it
   down rather than working around it.
5. **Vanilla JS + Web Components, no SPA frameworks, no build step.** Immutable
   versioned asset paths (`v0/v0.1/v0.1.0/`). This is a firm house rule; if it
   blocks a design you believe in, argue the case explicitly.
6. **A strict CSP is in force** on the app page — no external scripts, no CDN
   fonts, no remote images. Everything self-hosted or inlined.
7. Code is Apache 2.0, docs are CC BY 4.0.

## What we think is wrong with the current design

This is our read, offered as a starting point — **not a list of tickets**. Disagree
freely; a design that argues the current one is fine in some respect is useful too.

1. **The key wall comes before any value.** A first-time visitor is asked for an
   OpenRouter key before they have seen the product do anything. There are sample
   voice notes, but they sit below the key panel and still need a key to run.
2. **Three competing right-edge tabs** — 💬 chat, 🧭 flow, ⚙ debug — with a
   "one pane at a time" rule that exists because three panes fighting for the same
   space was unworkable. That rule is a symptom, not a solution.
3. **The best feature is hidden.** Chat-driven control of the tool is the
   commercial differentiator and it is behind an edge tab most users will never open.
4. **The options screen asks too early.** "Do you want an infographic?" is asked
   before the user has seen a transcript, let alone an infographic.
5. **Results are a long vertical scroll** of three stacked cards. Nothing helps the
   user compare, work with, or act on what was produced.
6. **Cost appears late.** The maximum cost is quoted on the options screen; nothing
   before that sets an expectation, and the user is spending their own money.
7. **Mobile is an afterthought.** The nav disappears below 640px and the panes are
   fixed at 460px. WhatsApp voice notes are a *phone* artifact — this may be the
   single biggest miss in the current design.
8. **No sense of progress or place** during a pass beyond a progress rail.

## What we want from you

### Three designs that are different bets, not three themes

Each design should be a coherent answer to "what is this product actually for?" —
and the three answers should genuinely disagree with each other. Some directions
that would qualify as different bets (illustrative only, pick your own):

- **Conversation-first** — the chat *is* the product; the pass is something you ask
  for, and transcript/summary/infographic are artifacts in a thread.
- **Workspace/canvas** — the materials are objects on a surface you arrange, compare
  and export; the pipeline recedes.
- **Mobile-native, one-thumb** — designed for the phone where the voice note lives,
  everything else follows.
- **Zero-friction demo-first** — the product does a full pass on a sample before it
  asks for anything, and the key request arrives at the moment of proven value.
- **Progressive/wizard** — one decision per screen, no options page at all.

Three of these, or three others. What we do not want is the same flow with different
typography.

### Per design, deliver

1. **The bet** — one paragraph: what this design believes about the user, and what
   it is willing to sacrifice for it. Be explicit about the trade-off.
2. **The flow** — the journey from arrival to result, and where the key request,
   the privacy choice, and the cost expectation sit in it.
3. **Visual design** — mockups or wireframes at desktop *and* phone widths.
4. **A working prototype** — a real page under `website/design/<variant>/`, running
   against the real `window.__tool` API, good enough to put in front of a user. Not
   a clickable picture.
5. **What it breaks** — honestly. Which current capabilities get harder or
   disappear, and why that is an acceptable price.
6. **What you would measure** to know whether the bet paid off.

## The A/B mechanics — and one open question you must not answer alone

Four variants (current + three) need visitors distributed between them, stably (a
returning visitor sees the same one) and without a backend. Assignment is
straightforward: a random bucket on first visit, persisted in `localStorage`, with a
URL override for us to force a variant.

**Measurement is the problem, and it is a real conflict, not a detail.** The site's
promise — stated in its own footer — is *"nothing tracked, nothing stored
server-side"*. A/B testing normally means recording what people did and sending it
somewhere. We cannot quietly break that promise to satisfy our own curiosity.

Three honest options, all with costs:

- **(a) Local-only telemetry.** Counters stay in the visitor's browser; we surface a
  "what this session did" view they can read and voluntarily send us. Keeps the
  promise fully. Yields almost no data at scale.
- **(b) Opt-in anonymous beacon.** Explicit, unticked-by-default consent; coarse
  events only (variant, reached-transcript, opened-chat); no content, no key, no
  identifiers. Keeps the promise honest *if* the footer is updated to say exactly
  this. Yields real but biased data — the people who opt in are not typical.
- **(c) Qualitative only.** Moderated sessions, no instrumentation at all. Truthful
  and rich, but slow and small-n.

**This is Dinis's decision, not the designer's, and not mine.** Design all three
variants so they work under any of the three; do not assume (b) and build event
hooks into the core flow before the decision is made. Flag anything your design
depends on measuring.

## What "works better" has to mean, decided before we build

Pick the measures with us up front, so we cannot rationalise a favourite afterwards.
Candidates:

- Proportion of arrivals that see a completed transcript at all *(the headline)*
- Drop-off at the key request, and whether moving it changes that
- Proportion who run a second voice note *(did it earn a habit?)*
- Proportion who ever open the chat, and who use it to change something
- Time from arrival to first transcript
- Phone vs desktop completion, separately — the current design almost certainly
  loses on phone

A design that wins on "time to first transcript" but loses on "ran a second note"
has probably optimised the wrong thing. Say which measure your design is betting on.

## Practical notes

- Each variant lives at `website/design/<variant>/` and must pass the existing CSP
  and the live-site checks.
- Once a variant is real, it gets its own QA-to-docs journeys, so its screenshots
  and user-guide pages are generated the same way the current one's are
  (`tests/qa-to-docs/journeys.json`).
- Do not touch the `version` file — CI owns it.
- Work on a branch, follow `CLAUDE.md`, and record what you build in the reality doc
  in the same commit.
- Ask questions rather than guessing at intent. A wrong assumption discovered in
  week three is far more expensive than a question asked on day one.

---

## Why we are doing this

The product works. What we do not know is whether it works *well* — whether the
shape we happened to build first is anywhere near the best shape available. Three
serious alternatives, built well enough to test, is the cheapest way to find out
before we scale acquisition against a design nobody validated.
