# 06 — The ask: what to simulate, and what we will do with it

**version** v0.1.23 · **date** 8 August 2026

---

Produce a **markdown document** (or a small set of them) that simulates semantic graphs
over a realistic corpus of voice notes. Everything below is what makes that simulation
useful rather than decorative. Work through it in order — later parts depend on earlier
ones being concrete.

Write for a reader who will implement from it. Prefer showing the actual data over
describing what the data would be like.

---

## Part 1 — Invent the corpus

**One fictional organisation. 8–12 WhatsApp voice notes. Two or three speakers. Over
three to five weeks.**

Pick a domain with real internal vocabulary — a small consultancy, a building
contractor, a clinical research team, a football club's recruitment staff. Avoid
generic startup-speak; the whole point is words that mean something specific to *these*
people and nothing to anyone else.

For each note give:

```
id, date, speaker, to whom, duration, and the setting in one line
(walking to a meeting, in a car, after a call — it affects the speech)
```

Then the **transcript**, and it must sound like a real voice note:

- false starts, self-correction, "sorry, no, it was the Tuesday one"
- references to shared context never explained ("the thing with Marcus")
- names, numbers, dates, half-remembered figures, one figure corrected later
- at least one place where the speaker is genuinely unclear
- at least one term used inconsistently *by the same speaker* across two notes

**A simulation over clean prose will produce a graph that draws beautifully and teaches
us nothing.** The messiness is the requirement.

Then the **summary**, in our exact shipped format — three sections, no others:

```markdown
## Key points
## Actions
## In one sentence
```

See `data/summary-prompt.md` for the full rules. `## Actions` items take the form
`- [who] what`, which means the summary already carries an **actor** the concept model
has no relation for. Worth noticing.

## Part 2 — Extract from note 1, with nothing to anchor to

Free extraction — the only time it happens. Produce a concept scheme in the shape of
`data/concepts.json`, adapted as you see fit, and **state every field you added and
why**.

Show the concepts, their definitions, their scope notes and their relations. Then say
honestly:

- how many concepts one 90-second voice note actually yields;
- which of them are real domain concepts and which are artefacts of one recording;
- what you had to guess.

## Part 3 — Map note 2 against note 1's scheme

This is the heart of it. Walk the pipeline from
[document 05](v0.1.23__pack__05-engineering-review-of-the-briefs.md) **by hand**, and
show the intermediate state at each stage — not just the outcome:

1. what the exact matcher resolved,
2. what the **lexical** retriever proposed as top-5 candidates, *including the wrong
   ones that scored well*,
3. what the model had to adjudicate, and what you would put in that prompt,
4. what the filters rejected,
5. what went to a person.

Then the **three-part output**, which is the product:

| | |
|---|---|
| **mapped** | matched an existing concept |
| **mapped approximately** | close, broader or narrower — record which |
| **did not map** | **the finding** |

## Part 4 — Break it on purpose

Include, and label, at least these:

- **The same idea, two names.** Two notes calling one thing different things. Show what
  the matcher does and whether it should have caught it.
- **One concept that should have been two.** A term the extractor collapsed that the
  organisation distinguishes.
- **A genuine lexical gap.** Something one speaker has a word for that the scheme cannot
  express — the equivalent of the pt-PT *idioma*/*língua* split in document 02. **This
  is not a defect**, and the output must be able to say so.
- **A confident wrong match.** The most dangerous failure: high lexical overlap, wrong
  concept. What in the pipeline catches it, and if nothing does, say so.
- **A concept from a misunderstanding.** The speaker was simply wrong about something.
  Should that enter the scheme?

## Part 5 — Run the whole corpus and show the accumulation

The question we most need answered:

> **At what point does the scheme stop changing shape on every new recording?**

Show the scheme's size and churn after each note — new concepts, merges, relabelings,
splits. If it is still growing linearly at note 12, say so plainly: that is a finding
about time-to-value, and it is better known now than after we build the matcher.

Then show the **final accumulated scheme**, and how many concepts and edges it holds. We
need that number: our own interface scheme is 15 concepts and 21 edges, and the layouts
we built for it may not survive 150 and 400.

## Part 6 — The chat operations

Show the edits a person would actually want after reading the report, **as an operation
sequence**, not as a rewritten scheme:

```
merge_concepts(a, b, keep: a, reason: "…")
add_alt_label(concept, "…", locale)
set_broader(child, parent)
split_concept(c, into: [x, y], reason: "…")
attach_definition(concept, "…")
reject_proposal(id, reason: "…")
```

Propose the vocabulary — the list above is a starting guess, not a specification. For
each operation say what it does, whether it is reversible, and what provenance it should
carry. These will become tools in the existing tiered chat registry (see document 02),
so a tier for each would be useful: `read`, `changes the scheme`, and so on.

## Part 7 — Propose the views

**This is the part that becomes our implementation specification.** Everything above
exists to make this part concrete.

Given the data you have just produced, what should a person actually see? Sketch it —
ASCII, mermaid, markdown tables, whatever carries the idea. For each view say what
question it answers and when it stops working.

Specific questions we need answered:

- **The unmapped report.** It is the most valuable output and it looks like failure. How
  is it laid out so it reads as insight rather than as the tool not working?
- **Does the graph view survive?** At 150 concepts, is a node-link diagram still useful,
  or does it become the hairball every graph visualisation becomes? If it does, what
  replaces it? (Our existing relations view draws one band per scheme with children
  fanned beneath the parent — fine at 15 concepts, unproven beyond.)
- **How is one recording shown against the scheme?** A voice note is a *slice* of the
  ontology. What does that slice look like?
- **How is change over time shown?** The scheme after note 12 is the asset. How does
  somebody see how it got there, and undo a step?
- **What does a person do next?** Every view should have an obvious action attached, or
  it is a poster.

Our three existing views (cards / matrix / relations, document 02) are available and the
matrix is currently the most useful. Say whether they transfer, and what a fourth would
need to be.

## Part 8 — Tell us what this changed

Close with what the simulation revealed that the briefs did not anticipate. That section
is likely to be the most valuable thing in the document, and it is the reason we are
doing this before writing code rather than after.

## Acceptance criteria

A design fails if it:

- **needs a backend**, a database or a vector store;
- **needs more than a handful of model calls per recording**, or a cost that grows with
  the size of the customer's ontology;
- **invents a data shape** unrelated to `data/concepts.json` without saying what changed
  and why;
- **only shows the happy path** — the failures in Part 4 are the point;
- **treats every difference between recordings as an error** — most differences are the
  model working;
- **produces a picture with no action attached to it.**

## Format

Markdown. Fenced JSON for data, fenced text or mermaid for diagrams. Tables where they
help. Long is fine; vague is not. Assume the reader implements from it and has the
constraints of document 01 in front of them.
