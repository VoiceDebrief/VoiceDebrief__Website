# 02 — What exists today: the concept scheme and its three views

**version** v0.1.23 · **date** 8 August 2026

---

This is the part of the two briefs that **has been built**, and it is the shape your
simulation output should fit into. Live at `/engineering/concepts/`.

## What it is

A concept scheme over the product's **own interface vocabulary** — the words on the
buttons. Fifteen concepts in three schemes. Meaning lives in a **definition**; each
culture attaches a **label**. It exists to answer "is this word right?" before a word
reaches users, and it was built to test the model on something small before pointing it
at customer data.

It is **deliberately not wired**: the app reads nothing from it, and the shipped strings
are untouched by it.

## The schema

The full file is `data/concepts.json`. One concept looks like this — this is real, not
illustrative:

```json
{
  "id": "locale-unreviewed",
  "scheme": "locale-state",
  "definition": "A locale carrying every string the source locale carries, which no human has yet checked on screen.",
  "scopeNote": "Nothing is missing. All four locales ship 53 of 53 keys — verified by the parity gate. The gap is verification, not coverage. A user may choose this locale; the product will never assign it to them.",
  "keys": ["core.localeDraft"],
  "current": { "en-gb": "draft", "en-us": "draft", "pt-pt": "rascunho", "pt-br": "rascunho" },
  "verdict": "change",
  "finding": "wrong-axis",
  "proposed": { "en-gb": "unreviewed", "en-us": "unreviewed", "pt-pt": "por rever", "pt-br": "não revisado" },
  "reasoning": "…",
  "broader": ["culture"],
  "related": ["locale-reviewed", "locale-planned"]
}
```

**Two groups of fields, and the distinction matters for your work:**

- **Core, and reusable for any scheme**: `id`, `definition`, `scopeNote`, `broader`,
  `narrower`, `related`, and per-locale labels.
- **Review-only, specific to reviewing interface words**: `verdict`, `finding`,
  `proposed`, `current`, `keys`, `reasoning`.

A customer ontology built from voice notes needs the core, does **not** need the
review fields, and needs fields this schema lacks — above all **provenance**: which
recording produced this concept, when, and with what confidence. Document 05 argues
this should be **one core schema with two profiles** rather than one shared file.
Your simulation is the thing that will show whether that is right.

Vocabulary is borrowed from SKOS (`definition`, `scopeNote`, `broader`, `narrower`,
`related`, `prefLabel`, `altLabel`) **without the RDF machinery** — no triple store, no
SPARQL. There is no backend to run them on.

## What the English pass found

Included because it is the clearest evidence that the model earns its keep, and because
the finding was not the one anyone predicted.

- **`draft` → `unreviewed`.** The brief supposed `draft` meant *incomplete coverage*.
  The data ruled that out: all four locales ship 53 of 53 keys. The flag actually gates
  on **review**. So `draft` names a stage of *authoring*, `incomplete` names *coverage*,
  and the concept is a state of *verification* — three different axes. The Portuguese
  `rascunho` was never a bad translation; it faithfully carried a word that was already
  pointing the wrong way.
- **`soon` → `planned`.** The product's own `culture.json` declares its register as
  *"no marketing register"*, and *soon* is a promise nothing backs.
- **Two privacy chips name a mechanism** inside a group asking where the audio goes,
  and one smuggles price into a privacy control. Marked *decide*, not changed, because
  they are claims made to users.
- **One thing that is not a defect**: pt-PT uses *idioma* for the setting and *língua*
  for the tongue that was spoken. English uses one word for both. Portuguese is being
  *more* precise — a genuine lexical gap, recorded rather than forced.

That last case is the one to keep in mind while simulating: **difference is usually the
model working, not a defect.** A mapping report that flags every divergence is useless.

## The three views, which your output has to render in

Built deliberately as three, because choosing how to look at this was half the exercise.
Whatever your simulation produces should be displayable in at least one of them — or
you should say clearly why a fourth view is needed.

### Cards — "is this word right?"
Definition first, scope note, then labels per culture, the proposal, and the reasoning.
Good for deciding one concept. Poor for seeing the whole.

### Matrix — "where do these part company?"
One row per concept, one column per culture. **The best of the three for this data.**
Divergence is legible without reading: a row differing in all four cells reads instantly
as healthy variation; a row agreeing in all four *and being wrong* reads as the
expensive case where every language inherited one error.

### Relations — the graph
Broader/narrower drawn as solid edges, related as dashed arcs, one band per scheme.
Honest about its own limits: **15 interface terms induce 21 edges**. That is thin, and
it is the reason a graph-divergence check is not worth building at this scale yet.

**Your corpus will produce a much denser graph than ours**, which is exactly why the
simulation is needed before the visualisation is designed. A layout that reads well at
15 nodes and 21 edges may be useless at 150 and 400.

## The drift gate, as a pattern worth copying

Because the scheme is separate from the shipped strings, it can drift — and a review
document describing strings that changed months ago is worse than none, because it is
confidently wrong. So every concept records what actually ships, and a CI test asserts
those recordings against the real locale files. It has been proven to fail when a
string moves.

The equivalent question for customer ontologies: **when a recording is re-processed, or
a concept is edited through chat, what stops the scheme and the corpus disagreeing?**
Worth addressing in your simulation.

## Patterns already established in this product

Four, all relevant, all already shipping — reuse rather than reinvent:

1. **Record, don't block.** When CI detects the UI has changed, it updates the baseline
   and writes a dated log entry naming the commit, rather than failing. A threshold
   cannot tell a redesign from a regression. Same logic will apply to mapping reports.
2. **Edits are reversible and attributed.** The chat can rewrite the summary; the page
   then shows *"✎ edited by the assistant · restore the original"*. Operation,
   provenance, undo — already working.
3. **Tools are tiered.** The chat's 13 tools each declare a tier: `read`,
   `changes materials`, `changes settings`, `spends money`. Ontology operations belong
   in that registry with the same vocabulary.
4. **Drafts are chosen, never assigned.** An unreviewed translation is offered but never
   auto-selected. The equivalent: a machine-proposed concept should be visible and
   marked, not silently absorbed into the customer's scheme.
